Your model is not confused by the questions. It understands them, and then gets stuck. There are two reasons, and you fix both today:

  1. It only thinks once. It reads the question, works out what is being asked, and then has to start writing straight away. It never gets a second pass to work out the answer.
  2. It has very little space to store facts. Everything a model learns is kept as numbers inside it. Only one small part of the model, called the feed-forward, is where facts are stored. In your model that part is tiny: 18,624 numbers, about 1.5% of the whole model. It's like trying to fit notes for twenty school subjects onto one sticky note. Facts get squashed on top of each other, so the model can't find them again when it needs them.

What Is a Transformer?

The kind of model you have been building all term

A Transformer is a type of AI model. ChatGPT is a Transformer too — just a much, much bigger one. Its job is always the same: read some text and guess the next word piece. Repeat that guess over and over, adding each new piece to the end, and you get a whole answer.

Your Transformer has three parts. The text goes through them in this order:

The three parts of your model

PartName in the codeWhat it does
1. Dictionary in token_embedding_table, position_embedding_table Turns each word piece into a list of numbers, and records where it sits in the sentence.
2. The thinking part sa_head (attention) and ffwd (feed-forward) Works out what the question means and what to say. This is the only part that does any reasoning.
3. Dictionary out lm_head Turns the final numbers back into a guess for the next word piece.

Where does a Transformer keep what it learns?

Inside the model there are millions of numbers, called parameters. During training, the model makes a guess, checks it against the real text, and nudges every number a tiny bit so it will be less wrong next time. After thousands of guesses, those numbers hold everything the model has learned.

There is no other memory. The model has no notebook, no database, and no copy of the book it read. If a fact is not stored in its numbers, the model does not know it.

So the size of each part matters. If the part that stores facts has very few numbers, it can only hold a few facts, however long you train it. That is your model's problem today, and the rest of this session shows you how to fix it.

1. Your Whole Transformer Is Two Lines

Find them before you change anything

Open attention_engine.py and find the class called PredictionEngine. Inside it is a method called forward. That is the code that runs every time the model makes a guess. In it are these two lines:

attention_engine.py — inside PredictionEngine.forward
x = x + self.sa_head(self.ln1(x))   # focus, then add the result back in
x = x + self.ffwd(self.ln2(x))      # think about it, then add that back in too

These two lines are the whole thinking part of your Transformer. Everything else in the file either turns words into numbers or turns numbers back into words. These two lines are the only place where your model works anything out.

  • Line 1, sa_head, is attention. It lets each word look at the words before it.
  • Line 2, ffwd, is the feed-forward. It adds facts the model learned in training.
  • ln1 and ln2 tidy the numbers to a sensible size before each step.
  • x = x + ... means “keep what you already had, and add the new result on top”.

What those two lines actually do

Definition: one layer

One attention step (look along the row) plus one feed-forward step (take facts off the shelf) is called a layer. The number of layers in a model is called its depth.

Your model has a depth of one. It looks once, thinks once, and then has to guess the next word. For comparison, GPT-2 has 12 layers and GPT-3 has 96.

2. Why One Round Is Not Enough

Round two does not start from the beginning

This is why more layers help.

When the students look along the row a second time, they don't read the original words again. They read each other's updated notepads, with everything worked out in round one. So round two starts where round one finished, instead of starting again from scratch.

That means six layers do more than repeat the same work six times. Each round builds on the one before it. A fact that was out of reach in round one can be on a notepad by round three.

Illustration — how understanding builds up over six rounds

Here is one way six rounds could work for “What is photosynthesis?” Step through it one round at a time.

1 Look: the word photosynthesis notices What is sitting in front of it. Think: its note now reads somebody is asking me for a definition.
2 Look: every other student reads that note. Think: the whole row now knows a definition is coming, not a yes-or-no answer.
3 Think: with the type of question settled, the feed-forward can bring in what it knows about the topic — plants, sunlight, leaves, green.
4 Look: those facts get shared along the row. Think: they are sorted into what belongs in a definition and what is merely related.
5 Think: a shape for the sentence appears — name the thing, say what it does, say what it needs.
6 Only now does the model pick the first word of its answer, with all five earlier rounds behind it.

4. The Three Numbers That Change That

Three settings decide how big the thinking part of your model is. Try out the first two here before you change them in the code.

4.1  n_embd — how wide the model is

n_embd is how many numbers describe one word piece. In the study group, it is how many lines fit on one student's notepad. Right now there are 96 lines, and they must hold everything about that word piece at once: what it means, which of the twenty subjects it belongs to, what usually comes next, whether it is a noun or a verb, and whatever attention just copied in. That was enough for four physics topics. For twenty subjects the notepad fills up, and new notes get written over old ones.

4.2  n_head — how that width is sliced up

Attention is split into heads. Each head looks for a different kind of link between words. For example, one head might link a question word like “why” to the topic of the question.

The heads do not get extra numbers of their own. They share out the numbers the model already has: head_size = n_embd ÷ n_head. More heads means the model can look for more things at once, but each head has fewer numbers to work with. Fewer heads means fewer things, each in more detail. Try it below, and watch the parameter count as you do.

Press each head button in turn. The parameter count does not change. Adding heads costs nothing: four heads of 64 and eight heads of 32 use the same 256 numbers, just cut up differently. Only the width (n_embd) changes the cost.

Most real models give each head between 32 and 128 numbers. At 96 wide with 4 heads you had 24 per head, which is too few. Today you go to 256 wide with 8 heads, which gives each head 32.

4.3  The feed-forward — where facts actually live

Find class FeedForward in attention_engine.py. Right now 96 numbers go in, it stays 96 numbers wide in the middle, and 96 come out.

Here is how a feed-forward stores facts:

  1. The first nn.Linear spreads each word's numbers out into a middle layer.
  2. Think of each number in the middle as a detector for one thing the model has learned to recognise. One might switch on for “this is about a chemical reaction”. Another might switch on for “a unit like metres comes next”.
  3. The second nn.Linear then decides what to write on the notepad, based on which detectors switched on.

So the width of the middle is how many separate things one layer can recognise. Yours is 96.

This is why your model can't hold on to what it learned. Training did teach it facts about twenty subjects. But it only has 96 detectors to keep them all in, so each detector has to cover many unrelated facts at once. The facts blur together, and when the model needs one for an answer, it can't pick it out clearly. More detectors means each fact gets clearer space of its own.

Step 2: Replace all ten lines

  • Select from class PredictionEngine(nn.Module): down to and including self.lm_head = ... — exactly the ten lines above.
  • Replace the whole lot with this:
attention_engine.py — the NEW code
class TransformerBlock(nn.Module):
    """One round of look-and-think.

    These are the same two lines the model always ran. Giving them a
    name is what lets us ask for six of them instead of one."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)   # tidy up before attention
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)   # tidy up before thinking
        self.sa_head = MultiHeadAttention(n_head, n_embd // n_head)
        self.ffwd = FeedForward(n_embd)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.sa_head(self.ln1(x))   # focus, then add the result back in
        x = x + self.ffwd(self.ln2(x))      # think about it, then add that back in too
        return x


class PredictionEngine(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.token_embedding_table = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, n_embd)
        self.position_embedding_table = nn.Embedding(block_size, n_embd)
        # Six rounds, one after another. Round 2 reads what round 1 wrote,
        # which is the whole reason six beats one.
        self.blocks = nn.Sequential(*[TransformerBlock() for _ in range(n_layer)])
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(n_embd)  # one last tidy before guessing
        self.lm_head = nn.Linear(n_embd, vocab_size)

Look closely at TransformerBlock.forward. Its two lines are exactly the lines that were in PredictionEngine.forward, comments and all. You haven't written any new logic. You've just put the old logic in a named box.

What a six-times-bigger model costs to train

Your model is about six times bigger, but training is about eleven times slower. That's because each of the 14,000 training steps now does about ten times as much maths: six layers instead of one, each with a much wider feed-forward.

Advanced Task: Open The Model And Add It Up

A .pth file is not magic. It is a dictionary of named lists of numbers, and you can open it and look. Rebuild the table from Section 7 yourself, straight from the file.

count_it.py — start from this
import torch

saved = torch.load("qa_model_v6.pth", map_location="cpu")
print("pieces in the file:", len(saved))

total = 0
for name, numbers in saved.items():
    total += numbers.numel()
    print(f"{name:52s} {tuple(numbers.shape)!s:20s} {numbers.numel():>9,}")

print("\ntotal:", f"{total:,}")

You will not get 7,951,872. You will get 11,097,600, and that gap is the whole point of this task.

  3. Explain in a sentence why it sits in the saved file but does not count as a parameter.

Then group the rest and check you can reproduce all four rows of the Section 7 table: 3,151,872 for the dictionary, 3,153,408 for the feed-forward, 1,574,400 for attention, and 72,192 for everything else.

⚙ Troubleshooting & FAQs

The parameter count is not 7,951,872

Activity 4 has a table of the common wrong numbers and what each one means. Work through that before anything else. The downloaded model will not load until this number is exactly right.

AttributeError: 'PredictionEngine' object has no attribute 'ln1'

Step 2 of Activity 2 is done but Step 3 is not. ln1 now lives inside TransformerBlock, so PredictionEngine.forward can no longer reach it. Replace those two lines with self.blocks(x) and self.ln_f(x).

RuntimeError: Error(s) in loading state_dict — a wall of red text

Read the first two lines of it, not all of it. If it says Missing key(s) with names like blocks.3.sa_head..., your model has fewer layers than the file expects — Activity 1 or 2 did not take. If it says size mismatch, your n_embd or feed-forward is wrong. Either way, run Activity 4 and fix the count first.

I am loading qa_model_v5.pth and it fails

Expected. Last session's model has one layer and 96 numbers per piece; today's code wants six layers and 256. They cannot be swapped. For the Activity 6 comparison, run talk_to_your_engine.py qa_model_v5.pth before you make today's changes, or keep a copy of the Session 2 folder.

ValueError or a shape error mentioning heads

n_embd must divide exactly by n_head. 256 ÷ 8 is 32, which is fine. 256 ÷ 7 is not a whole number, and neither is 96 ÷ 8 — so if you changed one of the two numbers and not the other, this is why.

The new model still gets things wrong

It will. 7,951,872 numbers is tiny next to anything you have used online, and it has read one book about twenty subjects. What you should see is answers that hold together for a whole sentence, which the Session 2 model mostly could not do. Being right more often is what Sessions 4 and 5 are for.
